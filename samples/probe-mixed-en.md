# Reichsbank and Goldmark — a mixed probe, English-led

This probe is bilingual, and not by section but inside the sentence.
**The base language is English**, and German words, whole German sentences,
German quotations and German number conventions lie inside it. Its
counterpart `probe-mixed-de.md` turns the direction around: there German
is the base and the English lies inside. The hardest places in this text
are not the numbers and not the abbreviations, but the points where each
language reads the same characters differently.

## Two number conventions in one text

The German report gives the entry as 1.234,50 Mark. The English abstract of
the same line writes 1,234.50 marks. It is the same sum, and reading one
spelling by the other convention moves it by a factor of a thousand.

It goes on like that. The appendix lists 50.000 Einheiten in one column and
50,000 units in the next; the footnote gives 0,75 Prozent and 0.75 per
cent. One memorandum speaks of 2,5 Millionen, the minutes of 2.5 million,
and both mean two and a half million. A third paper writes 1,5 and means
one and a half; a fourth writes 1,500 and means fifteen hundred.

**Nothing is guessed here.** Which convention holds is said by where the
line came from, not by the language of the voice.

## False friends among the number words

The source speaks of eine Billion Mark. In a German text that is a million
millions, while an English billion since the 1970s is a thousand millions —
and the difference is the thousandfold. The same trap sits in eine
Milliarde, which English has no plain word for, in ein Dutzend against a
dozen, and in der Zentner against the hundredweight.

The measures collide too. The distance is 3,5 km in the German print and
3.5 km in the English one. The weight is 6,35 kg or 14 lbs; the height is
1,83 m or 6 feet. The train does 113 km/h, that is 70 mph. Outside it is
20 °C, that is 68 °F. Nothing is converted that the text does not convert
itself.

## Terms that stay in their language

The Reichsbank held its Deckung in securities, and the Zollverein had
settled the Freihandel question long before 1871. The
Reichsschuldenverwaltung administered the debt, the Währungsumstellung of
1924 ended the inflation, and the Zahlungsbilanzausgleich was never
achieved at all. Words
like Realpolitik, Weltpolitik and Sonderweg stay as they are in an English
sentence, and so do Zeitgeist, Schadenfreude and Kindergarten, which no
longer feel foreign.

Nobody translates Reichskanzler into chancellor of the empire, and nobody
pronounces Nürnberg as though it were English, merely because the sentence
around it is.

## Whole German sentences inside English prose

Here the text changes language and changes back. Der Reichskanzler erklärte
am 3. April 1926 vor dem Reichstag, die Währung sei gesichert. The English
account of the same session calls the claim premature.

Die Reichsbank hielt ihre Reserven in Gold und Devisen, und sie gab Noten
nur gegen Deckung aus. Das war die Regel, und sie wurde zweimal gebrochen.
After 1931 nobody pretended otherwise.

Wer die Zahlen des Jahres 1913 mit denen von 1925 vergleicht, muss die
Währungsumstellung herausrechnen, sonst vergleicht er Papier mit Gold. That
is the sentence every later statistician has had to repeat.

Und noch ein Satz, der mitten im englischen Absatz steht, damit der Wechsel
nicht nur an einer Stelle geprüft wird: Die Zölle brachten 1912 rund 700
Millionen Mark ein, die Verbrauchssteuern etwas weniger.

## Rulers across language borders

The year 1888 is called das Dreikaiserjahr, the year of three emperors:
Wilhelm I. died in March, Friedrich III. in June, and Wilhelm II. reigned
until 1918. In English these are William I, Frederick III and William II;
in French Guillaume II. The same men, three spellings, three
pronunciations — and the choice depends on whose voice is speaking.

The other way round: Henry VIII stands in a German sentence as Heinrich
der Achte or as Henry the Eighth, and the text does not say which.
**That is a question and not a rule.** Charles V ruled as Karl V.,
Elizabeth I appears as Elisabeth I., and Pope Pius XII is Pius XII. in both.

## Umlauts, the sharp s, and long compounds

The places are Köln, München, Nürnberg, Württemberg, Zürich and Basel. The
family was von Schäffer-Löwenthal; the street was Große Bleichen; the
measure was a Maßnahme and the size a Größe. The word Straße carries the
sharp s, Strasse does not, and both appear in the sources.

Then the compounds, which no English speaker divides correctly at first
sight: Reichsschuldenverwaltung, Zahlungsbilanzausgleich,
Währungsumstellung, Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft. Every one of them
is a single word, and every one of them has to be read as one.

## German abbreviations in English prose

The ports are meant, z. B. those with a harbour, u. a. Hamburg und Bremen.
Vgl. Bd. II, S. 112, and compare it with p. 112 of the English edition.
The sum was 27 Mio. Mark, d. h. more than the budget, ca. twice the
estimate. The series ran from the 18. Jh. to the 20. Jh., and the entries
are marked Rm. for Reichsmark, M. for Mark and Pf. for Pfennig.

In one sentence both systems stand side by side: the list names u. a.
Hamburg, incl. Bremen, bzw. respectively Lübeck.

## Words that look the same and are not

The German word *Gift* means poison, not a present. *also* means therefore,
not as well. *Rat* is counsel, *Rate* an instalment, *Brand* a fire, *fast*
means almost, *Herb* is dry wine, *Stern* a star, *Mist* manure, *Kind* a
child, *Hut* a hat, *Rock* a skirt, *Bank* is both a bench and a bank, and
*Boot* is a boat. *Links* means left, and the Links in a document are
Verweise.

A listener hears from the pronunciation which language is meant. A reader
does not — and a speech model reads.

## Quotations that keep their language

The Reichsbank's own report of 1924 put it plainly:

> Die Währung ist nur so viel wert wie das Vertrauen, das ihr
> entgegengebracht wird, und dieses Vertrauen lässt sich nicht drucken.

The English sentences before and after it stay English. The quotation stays
German, with its 1.000 Mark and its 0,75 Prozent, and it is neither
translated, nor smoothed, nor brought into English number spelling.

The same holds for Keynes, quoted in a German edition as: Das Gold gehöre
nach London, denn dort werde es im Ernstfall gebraucht — a sentence written
in English, quoted in German, and now read in an English text.

## Dates that can be either

The order is dated 3.4.1926 in the German file and 4/3/1926 in the American
copy. In London the second reading is the fourth of March; in Berlin the
first is the third of April. The archive writes 1926-04-03, and that is
unambiguous. A letter carries 3. IV. 26, a telegram 3 Apr 26, a register
Apr. 3rd, 1926.

The season 1907/08 is der Winter 1907/08 in the German print and the
1907/08 season in the English original. The same characters, the same
meaning, two readings — and a third if two separate years are meant.

## Lists in two languages

The order rested on three things:

- the Deckung, which never fell below a third,
- the Diskontsatz, raised to 7 Prozent in 1907;
- and the belief, dass die Mark halten würde.

Drei Reformen folgten einander:

1. the Bankgesetz of 1875
2. die Währungsumstellung von 1924
3. the Reichsbankgesetz of 1939

## What a reading version cannot do here

A reading version resolves meaning. It can turn `1.234,50 Mark` into one
thousand two hundred and thirty-four marks fifty, and `3.4.1926` into a
date once its origin is known.

What it cannot do is the pronunciation. An English speech model says
Reichsschuldenverwaltung wrong however it is written out, and Nürnberg
wrong whether it keeps its umlaut or loses it. That is the pronunciation
list's work, and where even that is not enough, the only remaining answer
is not to voice a mixed text in one piece. This limit belongs to the probe:
it shows where the reading version stops and another tool has to begin.
